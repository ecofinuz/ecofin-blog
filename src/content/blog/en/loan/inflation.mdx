## What Is Inflation?

**Inflation** is the general rise in the prices of goods and services over time. In other words, it is the decline of money's purchasing power. There is a big difference between 1,000,000 soums in 2026 and 1,000,000 soums in 2020. Over six years, the purchasing power of that money has fallen noticeably.

Uzbek comedian Mirshakar Fayzulloyev has a joke about this. Ten years ago, if he entered Korzinka with 200,000 soums, he could barely carry all the bags. Now he leaves with one small bag.

In more formal language, during inflation the nominal value of money - the number printed on it - does not change, but its real value, or purchasing power, falls.

Calculating inflation is actually very time-consuming. Tracking prices and people's product choices across the whole economy is not easy. Countries use the concept of a **consumer basket** and CPI to monitor inflation. This is a large topic and deserves its own post.

---

Inflation is usually measured in two ways: **month-over-month** compared with the previous month, and **year-over-year** compared with the same month in the previous year. These indicators make it easier to calculate inflation between two periods. Inflation calculators work on the same basis. The Central Bank of Uzbekistan publishes this data every month.

## How Does It Work?

Prices rise for several reasons. The two main ones are:

**Demand-pull inflation** happens when there is a lot of money in the economy and too few goods. Imagine Uzbekistan's national football team plays the United Arab Emirates at Bunyodkor Stadium. The stadium holds a maximum of 34,000 spectators. Millions want to watch the match, and hundreds of thousands want to go to the stadium.

Demand is far higher than the number of tickets. In that situation, ticket prices naturally rise. A limited good is wanted by too many people. A 100,000 soum ticket rises according to demand and stops rising only when it reaches a price buyers are no longer willing to pay.

That is the theory. In reality, official ticket offices may not change prices. Instead, street resellers buy cheap tickets and sell them at a markup. Economically, the logic is clear; ethically, it is another question. People who try to travel to the regions by taxi before a holiday know this too: few cars, many passengers, higher fares.

This is also why the price of gold does not simply fall. Gold is a limited resource, but demand for it is high. That is why personal finance experts often recommend holding some gold.

At the national level, if the Central Bank prints more money or credit becomes cheaper, people and companies have more money and spend more. Aggregate demand rises. Production, however, does not always adjust quickly, so prices rise. Governments usually have better tools for controlling this type of inflation.

---

**Cost-push inflation** happens when production becomes more expensive. It is especially important when the prices of basic inputs change: fuel, raw materials, food, or labor. If these costs rise, businesses add them to final prices.

For example, after the war in Iran began in February, the Strait of Hormuz was closed. About 20% of the world's oil was caught in the blockade, and oil prices rose sharply. This affected goods and services worldwide. Airline tickets became more expensive too.

In Uzbekistan, higher methane or gasoline prices affect almost every good. Every household and business needs fuel. Production, logistics, taxis - everything gets more expensive. Demand-pull inflation usually affects a specific sector, but cost-push inflation affects the whole economy. Unfortunately, governments have weaker tools against this type of inflation.

## How It Affects Ordinary People

Inflation does not affect everyone equally. It is negative for everyone, because purchasing power is falling. But it hits three groups especially hard:

**#1. People holding cash savings.** Money kept at home or in a low-interest account loses value every year. This is true even if the money is in US dollars. The dollar also has inflation like any other currency. People keep savings in dollars because it loses value more slowly than the soum and is considered more reliable.

Inflation also explains why people in Uzbekistan are so attached to real estate. A person with money looks for a reliable investment that can resist inflation. Unfortunately, the stock market is still not well developed in our country. The culture of buying and storing gold is also not well formed. Partnership-based business and investment, such as mudarabah, is still not popular. The only familiar options left are land and housing.

**#2. People whose salaries are not indexed.** If your salary grows more slowly than inflation, you are effectively earning less even if the number looks the same. Suppose annual inflation averages 10%, but your monthly income grows by 8%. In real terms, you are losing money. Inflation hits low-ranking public-sector workers especially hard.

**#3. People with fixed income.** Pensions, benefits, stipends, or fixed rent income do not automatically rise with inflation. Why does the government increase pensions, benefits, and stipends by 10-15% almost every year? Inflation is one of the reasons.

Who benefits from inflation? Borrowers - if the interest rate is lower than inflation. In real life, banks almost never set interest rates below inflation.

This usually happens between two individuals. Suppose you lent a relative 10,000,000 soums in January 2025 and received it back one year later. People rarely index personal loans to inflation. Because money lost value during that year, you lost money by lending.

## Loans and Inflation

The connection between loans and inflation is unclear to many people, but it is not hard to understand. Inflation helps determine whether interest rates are low or high.

Suppose you took a 10,000,000 soum loan in 2020. Over five years, inflation averaged around 11%. That means the soums you repay later are worth less in real terms than the soums you received in 2020. The bank receives the same nominal amount, but its purchasing power has fallen.

That is why banks account for inflation when setting interest rates. In simple mathematical terms, the Fisher formula says:

```text
r = R - pi
```

If a loan is issued at 24% and inflation is 10%, the bank's real return is about 14%. In Uzbekistan, the Central Bank's policy rate is currently 14%. Inflation is around 7%. The real rate is roughly 7%. That is good for the bank and expensive for the borrower.

When TBC Bank gives you a consumer loan at 49%, by this logic it earns about 42% in real terms. In reality, the full Fisher formula also includes risk. We will discuss that in the next post.
