When people talk about credit, most of them discuss the [interest rate](/en/loan/how-rates-are-determined/). Is 24% good? Is 30% expensive? Which bank should you borrow from, and under what conditions?

But there is a deeper question that has troubled philosophers and religious scholars for thousands of years: **is credit itself fair?** More precisely, **is it right to [lend money and charge interest](/en/loan/when-debt-works/) on top of it?**

These questions are ancient. [Interest](/en/loan/simple-and-compound-interest/), and the question of money in general, is one of humanity's oldest financial disputes. Different philosophers, scholars, religious figures, and governments have answered it differently in different periods.

This topic is very deep, full of contradictions and uncertainty, and requires deep religious knowledge. So, of course, it cannot be covered in one article. I deliberately did not write about the views of different religions on interest in this article. Honestly, I do not have enough knowledge for that.

If religious questions about interest matter to you, ask scholars who specialize in religious economics.

That is why, in this article, I will focus only on Aristotle and modern economists.

## What Is Money?

What is money? Money is not only the banknote in your pocket or the money on your card. For money to work, three conditions must hold:

1. Money must be a medium of exchange.
2. Goods must be easy to express in money.
3. The value of money must be stable.

In other words, when you go to a store, the seller must accept the banknote you give, both of you must recognize its unit of value, and you must trust that this will still be true tomorrow. If you notice, money has always been built on trust.

In different periods, money has appeared in different forms: gold, salt, different kinds of shells, metal coins, paper, and sometimes even [wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tally_stick). Today, it is paper money and electronic money. In the future, maybe [bitcoin](https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitcoin). Where there is no money, say in prisons, cigarettes perform the function of money.

The original nature of money is this: **money is not a commodity**. _Money is only a medium of exchange_.

Whatever people and states accept and use as money becomes money. Because this system is built on trust, the state has placed strict control over money. That is why counterfeiting the Uzbek soum is punished very harshly.

If people in any society lose trust in money for different reasons - inflation, fake money, and so on - the economy begins to break down, and a major crisis happens.

So money is not a commodity. It is a medium of exchange that has gained society's trust. This exact difference is what caused the disputes around credit.

## An Ancient Debate

Is it right to give money and charge interest on top of it? In short: **does money give birth to money?** Yes or no?

Let us divide those who say "yes" into people who see money as an opportunity or commodity (**the Opportunity Group**), and those who say "no" into people who see money as a tool (**the Function Group**). Personally, I am on the second side.

The Opportunity Group describes money as an opportunity, a commodity, and sometimes even a service. In other words, a wealthy person has the right to profit from a debtor because he gave that person money. Like rent. You rent your property or your car to someone, and you _rightfully_ earn income from it.

The Function Group sees money as a medium of exchange. Money does not have value like other goods. Bread, clothing, a lathe - all of them have their own value. Money has no value of its own. Its value exists only in its exchange function. One person giving money to another does not automatically create additional value.

Below, we will start with the Function Group and then look at the Opportunity Group in modern economics.

## Aristotle

The earliest representative of the Function Group was the ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle. Around 350 BCE, in the first book of his work "Politics", Aristotle wrote the most famous argument against interest.

His idea was this: money is a medium of exchange. It exists only to buy goods. Money itself cannot create anything - it is "sterile". Below is his most famous passage translated into English:

> "Of all ways of getting wealth, the most hated, and with the greatest reason, is usury, lending money at interest. It makes profit not from the nature of money, but from money itself... For money was made for exchange, not to increase through interest. The word for interest, tokos, meaning the birth of money from money, is used as if money reproduced like a living creature, because what is born resembles its parents. That is why, of all ways of acquiring wealth, usury is the most unnatural."

How can a tool with no value of its own create value? By what logic can I give you 1,000,000 soums in a difficult moment and demand 1,100,000 soums back? Especially if I know you?

Some may say this is like rent. But rent also has its own conditions. First of all, the thing being rented out must be a commodity. Food, a service, or money is not rented out. They are only given to be used. In other words, if I take 10,000,000 soums from you, that money will not sit in my house. I will spend it in some way.

Also, if you rent an apartment from me and cannot pay this month's rent because you are struggling, you simply leave the apartment. Your debt does not become huge. I do not charge you penalties for every passing day.

People may say that you could have used the money yourself, and by lending it to someone else you are giving up those opportunities. In other words, the economic idea of [opportunity cost](/en/loan/the-true-cost-of-a-loan/#opportunity-cost). They also say the borrower may not return the money, so you are taking a fee for that risk too.

But wealthy people do not always have investment opportunities, apart from deposits. Believe me, many people's money is just sitting at home in a chest. Is it not better for that money to benefit someone than to lie at home without benefiting anyone? If the loan amount is large and the person asking for money is not close to you, the debt can always be formalized through a notary.

Now let us think bigger. Lending money at interest leads to class division and dissatisfaction in society. Imagine you are a medieval billionaire. Not an entrepreneur, not a scholar, not a merchant, but simply a moneylender who gives people loans at interest. You lend to peasants and craftsmen for different purposes. You lend with written receipts. If they cannot pay back the debt, you take their property, livestock, land, workshop. In modern terms, you are a bank.

Now imagine that a great disaster happens. A war begins, famine strikes, disease spreads. You survive. Debtors naturally cannot repay their debts. You take everything they have or buy it at very low prices. Do you think a people who have gone through so much suffering will not notice this or will not revolt?

Even today, during financial crises, the elite almost always becomes richer and gains more power. It is just a phenomenon hidden behind very difficult economic terms.

These ideas of Aristotle strongly influenced Western and Eastern thought for the next 2,000 years.

## Modern Economics

Let us admit it: money is power. A person with money has more connections and more opportunities. And when we say a person with money, we are not talking about the local rich person in your neighborhood, but about people who manage billions and trillions.

This elite lends to governments at interest, buys large companies, lends to corrupt generals and dictators in poor countries, and their people suffer afterward. Because of debt, the lands of entire peoples are taken away, and someone else's rules are imposed.

Since ancient times, almost all philosopher-scholars and religions have opposed interest. They called lending at interest evil. Aristotle, Plato, Christianity, Islam, and even Judaism. In Islam, interest is compared to declaring war against Allah and His Messenger.

The capitalist system in its current form began in late eighteenth-century England with the Industrial Revolution. Along with companies, states, and people, moneylenders also developed and grew stronger. They created banks and founded international organizations. If you ask the average economist inside today's system, he will praise the system and defend it.

Yet this is a system that protects people with money and makes them even more powerful. Uzbekistan is simply a small player that joined this system late. We never chose this system. We were forced to choose it.

Think about it: **how can everyone be in debt at the same time**? People have loans, the state's debt is growing, companies do not raise salaries because they have debt. If everyone is a debtor, who is lending? The people with money who are not mentioned in the media. They control the media too.

Economic knowledge about interest is used to protect the system, not to audit it. There is also an interest-free Islamic finance system that has worked for centuries and, scientifically, may still work today.

If you know English, I recommend the documentary [Money Masters (1996)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bm6oeRgxs0A) on this topic. It has mistakes, but it is a good documentary.

## A Rigid System

As long as banks make money by giving loans, and as long as powerful people remain in their positions because of this system, it will not change anytime soon. Not because it is unfair, unjust, and cruel, but because the people inside it benefit from it, and they will defend interest-bearing credit with everything they have.

Even if you tell people not to take credit, show that there are other paths, show that this is not a fair system, and present scientific evidence, those who benefit from the system will deny that evidence. They may even throw stones at me, saying, "Who are you anyway, just an ordinary student?"

So the system is very rigid. It is difficult to change. It is also difficult to achieve any practical result. As an individual, your best defense is to increase your knowledge and your wealth, and to refuse credit.
