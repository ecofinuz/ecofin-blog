In previous posts, we looked at [the true cost of a loan](/en/loan/the-true-cost-of-a-loan/) - the monetary cost shown in bank documents, the opportunity cost for you, and the psychological cost. After reading it, your view of credit may have changed. The real cost of credit is much higher than many people think, and it is not discussed enough.

That is true. But it is not the whole picture. At Ecofin, we always try to stay balanced, so we do not want to show only the bad side of credit. In this post, we will take one step away from credit itself and talk about debt.

In reality, debt itself is not only a bad thing. Debt is a tool. And like any tool, its value depends on how it is used.

As O'tkir Hoshimov wrote in _Notes in the Margin_:

> An axe in a killer's hand is a weapon of death. In a woodcutter's hand, it is a tool of labor. In a craftsperson's hand, it is a tool of art.
>
> A pen in an envious person's hand is a weapon of death. In a literate person's hand, it is a tool of labor. In a talented person's hand, it is a tool of art.

## Debt Is Not Bad

Historically, borrowing has been a normal part of society. In difficult times, or before the harvest, people borrowed from each other. During wars and famines, states borrowed from one another. For large projects, cities borrowed from other cities. Even today, people in our society borrow small amounts from each other until payday. Or they borrow from relatives for a longer period to pay for a wedding, a home, or a car.

In other words, in its most original and ideal form, debt is one person's help to another, built on trust. You probably would not lend money to your friend or relative at interest. At least I hope so.

Because debt is built on trust, it can also damage relationships. Imagine that you want to help a friend in a difficult moment and lend them 1,000,000 soums because you trust them. Then they stop answering your calls and messages. The anger and disappointment in that situation are not only about 1,000,000 soums. They are also about lost trust.

If we describe the ideal version of debt, it looks like this: one person helps another person in a difficult situation **without any interest**, then receives the money back after an agreed period. In that case, debt is a very good tool that increases trust between both sides and reduces hardship.

Notice the key phrase: without any interest. That means I should not give you 1,000,000 and demand 1,100,000 back. In that case, I become a _creditor_ or, in plain language, a _moneylender_.

## What About Credit?

Unfortunately, the situation described above is becoming less common today. Everyone has a family and household to support. Not everyone is financially comfortable. Trust and mutual responsibility have weakened in society, while people's desires have grown. On top of that, almost every family now has a consumer loan, car loan, or mortgage.

That is why people are more likely to get credit from online banks, microfinance organizations, or local moneylenders than to ask one another for debt. But as we saw in the previous post, [credit is not a good tool](/en/loan/the-true-cost-of-a-loan/). Its cost is far too high. Unfortunately, this credit is often spent on things that do not make much logical sense: phones, household appliances, weddings, and celebrations.

But people still need money. So what should they do? Credit should still remain the last resort. It makes sense to take credit only after relatives, friends, and neighbors cannot help. At the very least, you will not have to return more than you received. You will not carry the monthly fear of _"I have to pay the loan, otherwise they will charge penalties."_

Apart from medical treatment and emergency situations, there is one case where taking credit can make sense: investment.

## Investment

Will a loan that you repay with interest bring you future benefit or not? In other words, will that money **increase your wealth**, **grow your business**, **expand your knowledge**, or **raise your skills**? Notice that I did not write, "How will it make your life more comfortable?" or "What convenience will this loan create?"

Based on that, let us divide credit into **useful** and **harmful**.

**Useful credit** is credit taken for something that can create more money, knowledge, or opportunity in the future. For example:

- Education and professional development
- Starting or expanding a business
- Income-generating equipment
- Real estate that can increase in value

**Harmful credit** is credit taken for something that loses value or gives no return. For example:

- A new smartphone, if the old one still works
- Clothes and accessories
- Vacations and entertainment
- A new car, if transport is not truly necessary

Of course, the difference varies from person to person. A laptop bought on credit to learn a new profession and get a job is an investment. A laptop bought without any clear goal is simply waste. If Toshmat buys an iPhone 17 on a two-year loan just to show off, that is obvious foolishness. If Azamat, who works as a mobile videographer, buys an iPhone 17 to improve his work, that is an investment. You can see the difference, right?

Pause for a moment. The difference looks simple. But in practice, it is often not that easy to separate useful credit from harmful credit. A loan may look like an investment, but you need to think more deeply.

"This phone is for my work" - maybe. But the old phone also worked. Instead of buying a brand-new iPhone 17 on credit, could you buy a used iPhone 15 for half the price? Instead of an Onix or Tracker on credit, would a cheaper Nexia 3 or Cobalt be enough? Instead of a gaming laptop with an Nvidia GPU, would a simpler office computer work? One important pillar of financial literacy is the ability to _ask yourself these questions_ and _answer them honestly_.

Ask yourself: **how useful will this loan be for me in three years?** If the answer is clear and calculated, then the loan may be logical and useful. If the answer is "I do not know" or mostly emotional, stop and think again.

Below are two examples of this idea: one at a large scale and one at a smaller scale.

## Grameen Bank

In 1983, Bangladeshi economist Muhammad Yunus founded an unusual bank. At that time, the banking system in Bangladesh did not lend to poor people. The reason was simple: no collateral, no guarantees, no credit history. Many people were tenant farmers with no property. For banks, the profit was low and the risk was far too high. _Nothing personal, just business._

Yunus thought differently. He went to the poorest villages of Bangladesh and gave people - mostly women - very small amounts of money: microloans of $20, $50, $100, and so on. Without collateral. At relatively low interest rates. Only on the basis of trust. The goal was to help them start small business activity, not to buy a phone or washing machine. People who were tired of moneylenders used this opportunity as much as they could.

The result was different from what almost everyone expected. Repayment rates were **higher** than in commercial banks, because people used the money to start family businesses. Poor women used their reputation and the trust of their community as collateral before the bank. They traded goods, started handicrafts, and sold food.

At the national level, poor rural people found work, got out of debt, moved away from crime, and educated their children. The model began to spread to other countries. The results were so impressive for Bangladesh that Muhammad Yunus received the [2006 Nobel Peace Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/2006/yunus/facts/). He also gave a [TEDx talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6UCuWxWiMaQ&t).

In a similar way, Islamic banks lend to people and companies for good purposes without interest. Islamic finance and banking are topics for another series.

This is the strongest argument for the credit system: under the right conditions and for the right purpose, credit can lift people out of poverty. Credit can help people.

## The Laptop

This example is from my own life. During Covid-19, my older brother began studying IT. We were at home in quarantine, and he worked on an old computer released in 2009. We used a modem or USB device to connect to the internet. My brother studied Python and Django deeply and with real interest. He built projects.

By the summer of 2021, he was ready to get a job. But he needed a good laptop to start working. We had no money, and relatives could not help either. In the end, my brother bought a laptop for $700: a Dell Vostro 3500. On credit.

He got a job, and because his knowledge and motivation were strong, his salary grew quickly. Around that time, I also got into Westminster. Later, my brother bought a more powerful laptop for himself, and I took his old one. I studied Python, Django, and data engineering much more deeply. I built websites and used IT in finance.

Then I also found work in IT and finance. I worked at a startup, at Coca-Cola, and at the Central Bank. I am writing this post right now on that same laptop from five years ago. It still works very well.

During that time, my brother went to Germany and worked there, I graduated, we improved our financial situation, and thank God, we got back on our feet. I do not know what life would look like now if my brother had not gone into this field, bought that laptop, and started working.

That was exactly a useful investment. A $700 loan taken in the form of a laptop for knowledge and skills - not for gaming or entertainment - returned to our family as several tens of thousands of dollars over the years.

## Conclusion

Debt is not bad. Everyone goes through difficult times and may need financial help. Sincere, interest-free help is a very good thing. It can also improve trust and mutual responsibility in society.

Credit is usually bad. I do not recommend it to anyone. But sometimes taking credit becomes necessary. Even then, it should be used to invest in useful things.

Muhammad Yunus gave credit to women in Bangladesh - they fed their families, built businesses, and escaped poverty. My brother bought a laptop for $700, and we developed skills and built careers for ourselves.

In both cases, credit worked the same way: it brought a future opportunity into the present. The question should never be "Is credit good or bad?" The question should always be: **"Will this loan justify itself in the future?"**
