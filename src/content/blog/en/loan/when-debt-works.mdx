In previous posts, we looked at [the cost of credit](/en/loan/the-true-cost-of-a-loan/) - the interest cost shown in bank documents, plus the opportunity and psychological costs that most people ignore. After reading it, your view of credit may have changed. The real cost of credit is much higher than many people think, and it is not discussed enough.

That is true. But it is not the whole picture. At Ecofin, we always try to stay balanced, so we do not want to show only the bad side of credit. Credit is an inseparable part of the modern financial system, and however much we dislike or criticize it, we still need to see reality honestly.

In this post, we will take one step away from credit and talk about debt. The kind of debt people commonly understand. Credit is also debt, except you pay a price for it in interest.

In reality, debt itself is not only a bad thing. Debt is a tool. And like any tool, its value depends on how it is used.

As O'tkir Hoshimov wrote in _Notes in the Margin_:

> An axe in a killer's hand is a weapon of death. In a woodcutter's hand, it is a tool of labor. In a craftsperson's hand, it is a tool of art.
>
> A pen in an envious person's hand is a weapon of death. In a literate person's hand, it is a tool of labor. In a talented person's hand, it is a tool of art.

## Debt Is Not Bad

Historically, borrowing has been a normal part of society. In difficult times or before the harvest, people borrowed from each other; during wars and famines, states borrowed from one another; for large projects, cities borrowed from other cities.

Islamic finance has a [Salam contract](https://mezonkengashi.uz/salam-yoki-salaf.html). Historically, farmers could sell their harvest before it was ready. In practice, farmers borrowed money by pledging their future harvest. This mechanism worked well for farmers. Otherwise, why would Islamic finance permit _Bay al-Salam_?

Even today, people in our society borrow small amounts from one another until payday. Or they borrow from relatives and neighbors for medium or long periods to pay for a wedding, a home, or a car.

In its most original and ideal form, debt is one person's help to another, built on trust. You probably would not lend money to your friend or relative at interest. At least I hope so.

Because that trust exists, debt can also damage relationships. Imagine you want to help a friend in a difficult moment and lend them 1,000,000 soums because you trust them. Then they stop answering your calls and messages. The anger and disappointment in that situation are not only about 1,000,000 soums. They are also about lost trust.

There is a reason people have so many [proverbs about debt](https://myfarzand.uz/maqollar-qarz.html). Debt is not only a money tool. It is also a trust contract between two sides. You understand this when you lend with your hand and then chase with your feet.

Debt is not bad: one person helps another in a difficult situation **without any interest**, then receives the money back after an agreed period. In that form, debt is a very good tool that increases trust between both sides and reduces hardship.

Notice the key phrase: without any interest. That means I should not give you 1,000,000 and demand 1,100,000 back. In that case, I become a _creditor_ or, in plain language, a _moneylender_.

With credit, [interest and loan terms](/en/loan/simple-and-compound-interest/) are added on top of that help. Credit turns from a tool of trust into an instrument for making money. If you do not believe that, look at banks and moneylenders.

## What About Credit?

Unfortunately, the situation described above is becoming less common today. Everyone has a family and household to support. Not everyone is financially comfortable. Trust and mutual responsibility have weakened in society, and people's desires have grown.

On top of that, almost every family has a consumer loan, car loan, or mortgage.

For many reasons, people are more likely to get credit from online banks, microfinance organizations, or local moneylenders than to ask one another for debt. But as we saw in the previous post, [credit is not a good tool](/en/loan/the-true-cost-of-a-loan/). Its cost is far too high.

Unfortunately, such credit is often spent on things that do not make logical sense: phones, household appliances, weddings, and celebrations. That is why you should also calculate the [true cost](/en/loan/the-true-cost-of-a-loan/#opportunity-cost) before signing a loan agreement.

But people need money. So what should they do? There are many ways: save, buy later, ask a trusted friend or relative for debt, or sell livestock or household assets. Whatever happens, credit should remain the last resort.

It is logical to take credit only after relatives, friends, and neighbors cannot help. At the very least, you will not have to return more than you received. You will not carry the monthly fear of _"I have to pay the loan, otherwise they will charge penalties."_

Apart from medical treatment and emergency situations, there is one case where taking credit can make sense: investment. And it is a very specific case.

## Investment

Will a loan that you repay with interest bring you future benefit or financial loss? In other words, will that money **increase your wealth**, **grow your business**, **expand your knowledge**, or **raise your skills**?

Notice that I did not write, "How will it improve your life?" or "What convenience will this loan create?"

Based on that, let us divide credit into **useful** and **harmful**.

**Useful credit** is credit taken for something that can create more money, knowledge, or opportunity in the future. For example:

- Education and professional development
- Starting or expanding a business
- Spending on income-generating equipment, such as a sewing machine, machinery, or a laptop
- Real estate that can increase in value

**Harmful credit** is credit taken for things that lose value or give no return. For example:

- A new smartphone, if the old one still works
- Clothes and accessories, such as an iPhone 18 Pro Max
- Vacations and entertainment
- A new car, if transport is not truly necessary

Whether credit is useful or harmful obviously differs from person to person. A laptop bought on credit to learn a new profession and get a job is an investment. A laptop bought without any clear goal is simply waste.

If Toshmat buys an iPhone 17 on a two-year loan just to show off, that is obvious foolishness. If Eshmat, who works as a mobile videographer, buys an iPhone 17 to improve his work, that is an investment. You can see the difference, right?

Pause for a moment. The difference looks simple. But in practice, it is often not easy to separate useful credit from harmful credit. A loan may look like an investment, but you need to think more deeply.

"This phone is for my work" - maybe. But the old phone also worked. Instead of buying a brand-new iPhone 17 on credit, could you buy a used iPhone 15 for half the price? Instead of an Onix or Tracker on credit, would a cheaper Nexia 3 or Cobalt be enough? Instead of a gaming laptop with an Nvidia GPU, would a simpler office computer work? One important pillar of financial literacy is the ability to _ask yourself these questions_ and _answer them honestly_.

Ask yourself: **how useful will this loan be for me in three years?** If the answer is clear and calculated, then the loan may be logical and useful. If the answer is "I do not know" or mostly emotional, stop and think. Taking credit based on emotion is obvious short-sightedness.

Below are two examples of this idea: the first at a large scale, across society; the second personal and small-scale.

## Grameen Bank

In 1983, Bangladeshi economist [Muhammad Yunus](https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Yunus) founded a historically unusual bank: [Grameen Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grameen_Bank). At that time, the banking system in Bangladesh did not lend to poor people. The reason was simple: no collateral, no guarantee, no credit history, and no official income. Many people were tenant farmers with no property.

For banks, the profit from such people was low and the risk was far too high. _Nothing personal, just business._

Muhammad Yunus thought differently. He went to the poorest villages of Bangladesh, studied the situation, and gave people, mostly women, very small amounts of money: [microloans](https://finlit.uz/uz/articles/credit/microloan/) of $20, $50, $100, and so on. Without collateral and at relatively low interest rates. Only on the basis of social trust.

These microloans were given so people could start small business activity, and they were monitored at the community level. Not so they could buy a phone or washing machine. People tired of moneylenders used this opportunity as much as they could.

The result was different from what everyone expected. Repayment rates were **higher** than in commercial banks, because people used the money to start family businesses. Poor women used their reputation and the respect of their community as collateral before Grameen Bank. They traded goods, started handicrafts, and sold food.

Across Bangladesh, poor rural people found work, got out of debt, moved away from crime, and educated their children. The model began to spread to other countries. The results were so impressive for Bangladesh that Muhammad Yunus received the [2006 Nobel Peace Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/2006/yunus/facts/). He also gave a [TEDx talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6UCuWxWiMaQ&t).

In a similar way, Islamic banks lend to people and companies for good purposes without interest. Islamic finance and banking are topics for another series, God willing. Someone who understands Islamic finance deeply should write about it.

This is the strongest argument for the credit system: under the right conditions and for the right purpose, credit can lift people out of poverty. Credit can help people too. But as I said, only in very specific cases.

## The Laptop

This example is from our own life. I am 22 now, and my brother is 25. We both work in IT.

During Covid-19, my brother began studying IT. At that time, this field was only starting to enter Uzbekistan at scale, and young people who knew English were seriously starting to learn it. Everyone was at home anyway. Everyone had time. Many people were idle then.

We were at home in quarantine, and my brother studied IT on an old computer released in 2009. That PC did not even have Wi-Fi; we used a USB cable to connect to the internet. My brother studied [Python](https://mohirdev.uz/blog/python-dasturlash-tilining-xususiyatlari-va-imkoniyatlari/)/[Django](https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Django) deeply and with real interest, and built websites. He created a portfolio for himself.

By the summer of 2021, he was ready to get a job. But he needed a good laptop to start working. We had no money, and relatives could not help either. In the end, my brother bought a laptop for $700: a [Dell Vostro 3500](https://asaxiy.uz/product/noutbuk-dell-vostro-3500-intel-core-i5-1135g7-ddr4-4gb-hdd-1000gb-156-wva). On credit.

Within about a month, my brother got a job, started earning a salary, and because his knowledge and motivation were strong, he grew quickly at work.

Around that time, I also got into Westminster. Later, my brother bought a more powerful laptop for himself, and I took his laptop. I studied Python, Django, and Data Engineering much more deeply. I built websites and used IT in finance. This blog is also a fruit of those days.

Then I also found work in IT and finance. I worked at startups, Coca-Cola, and the Central Bank. I am writing this post right now on that Dell Vostro 3500 bought on credit five years ago. It still works reliably.

During that time, my brother went to Germany and worked there, I graduated, we improved our financial situation, and thank God, we got back on our feet. I do not know what life would look like now if my brother had not gone into this field, bought that laptop, and started working.

Notice that my brother bought the laptop because he needed it for work. Not in the first month of learning IT.

Again, it comes down to useful investment. This loan was an investment: in knowledge and skills, not in gaming or entertainment. The $700 loan taken for a laptop in 2021 returned to our family as several tens of thousands of dollars over the years. Since then, our family has never taken another loan. May we never take one.

## Conclusion

Debt is not bad. Everyone goes through difficult times and may need financial help. Sincere, interest-free help is a very good thing. It can improve trust and mutual responsibility in society. Historically, debt has been normal.

Credit is a distorted form of debt. I do not recommend it to anyone. But sometimes taking credit becomes necessary. If you take credit, it should be invested in useful things.

Muhammad Yunus gave credit to women in Bangladesh - they fed their families, built businesses, and escaped poverty. My brother bought a laptop for $700, and we developed skills and built careers for ourselves.

In both cases, the essence of credit was the same: it brought a future opportunity into the present. The question should never be "Is credit good or bad?" The question should always be: **"Will this loan justify itself in the future?"**
